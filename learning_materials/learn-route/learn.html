For anyone reading the news

Before you trust a camera count

Companies, agencies and researchers increasingly report what cameras and AI saw underwater. Five questions separate a solid claim from a shaky one. Each links to the module that explains it.

  1. Could the camera see?

    Murky water, low light and distance shrink how far a camera can see. Fewer sightings can mean worse visibility, not fewer animals.

    Module 02
  2. Was the AI tested on footage like this?

    A score from clear reef water says little about a murky harbour. Ask for results by condition and by site, not one number.

    Module 05
  3. How much does it miss, and how do they know?

    Every detector misses animals. Repeat surveys let you estimate how often, and correct for it.

    Module 09
  4. Did a person check what matters?

    Alerts about protected species and welfare flags should reach a trained person before anyone acts on them.

    Module 05
  5. What does the claim not show?

    “No fish detected” is not “no fish harmed”. A good finding says what it can’t tell you, and how big an effect the survey could have missed.

    Module 09

    For teachers, guides and organisers

    Teach with this

    Ready-to-run plans for a public session, a hands-on activity with the live cameras, and a five-week course. Everything is free and runs in a browser.

    One-hour public session

    Libraries, aquariums, community groups. Ages 14 and up. A projector and internet.

    1. 0–10 min. Run the ten-minute tour on the big screen. Let the room call out the distance and water type.
    2. 10–25 min. “Count, then detect” on a live reef camera (the activity in the next card).
    3. 25–40 min. Open Module 09’s “Seen versus there” demo. Ask: did the fish leave, or did the camera stop seeing them?
    4. 40–55 min. Apply the five questions to a real news story about wildlife cameras or AI.
    5. 55–60 min. One sentence each: what would you ask a company that says its outfall harms no fish?

    Count, then detect

    A 15-minute activity for pairs, using the Live ocean cameras. Works for classrooms and citizen-science groups.

    1. Open Live ocean and choose a reef camera in daylight.
    2. One person counts every fish they see in 60 seconds. The other notes water clarity: clear, hazy or murky.
    3. Press Detect animals and record what the detector reports over the next 60 seconds.
    4. Swap roles, repeat on a murkier camera, and compare.
    5. Discuss: where did people and the detector disagree, and why? Which count would you trust for a permit?

    Five-week course

    University or graduate students starting from scratch. About 20 hours of student time.

    • Week 1. Modules 01–02: images and water.
    • Week 2. Modules 03–04: classical tools and honest evaluation.
    • Week 3. Modules 05–06: robustness and neural networks.
    • Week 4. Modules 07–08: measurement and tracking.
    • Week 5. Module 09 and a capstone.

    Look for: results by condition and site; clip-level splits; two degradation models; placeholders flagged; at least one conditional finding with its uncertainty.

    Setting up the notebooks

    Each module has an Open in Colab button. Colab opens a copy from the public aquatic-guardian-learning-modules repository. Choose File, Save a copy in Drive first. Real wildlife frames load from that repository; the notebooks also make synthetic scenes so every answer can be checked against the truth. All nine run top to bottom on Colab’s free CPU, most in seconds and Module 06 in about a minute.

    Exercises print feedback instead of crashing, and each has a collapsed solution.

    Known simplifications

    Scenes and surveys are synthetic, so every answer can be checked against the truth. Attenuation coefficients, turbidity units and detection parameters are teaching values, not site measurements. Species parameters and welfare bands are placeholders, as in the project notebooks. Numbers quoted in the lessons come from test runs of the notebooks; the neural-network results can shift slightly between runs.

    Plain-language glossary

    Questions people ask

    Do I need to know how to code?

    No. The tour, the five questions and every module page work without code; the demos run in the page. Choose “Policy, no coding” as your starting point. The notebooks are there if you want to go further.

    Is the underwater footage in the lessons real?

    The demos use real wildlife frames (live-camera stills and NOAA public-domain photographs). The notebooks also build synthetic scenes so every graded answer can be checked against the truth. The Live ocean page shows live cameras.

    What do I need to run the notebooks?

    A free Google account and a browser. Press “Open in Colab” on any module, then File, Save a copy in Drive and Runtime, Run all. No installation or GPU is needed.

    Can I use this in my class or community group?

    Yes. “Teach with this” has a one-hour session plan, a printable activity, and a five-week course outline. Check the repository for its licence before redistributing material.

    Why does a course about cameras talk about permits?

    Because camera results end up in decisions about data centres, outfalls and fisheries. A count that ignores what the camera missed can make harm look like safety, or safety look like harm. Module 09 shows how to tell them apart.
