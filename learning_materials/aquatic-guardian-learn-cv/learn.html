  1. 0 m
    01Images are arrays of numbersoptional for your path›1.5 hPixels, channels, cropping, and the two bugs everyone writes.

    Every later step is arithmetic on these numbers. Most beginner mistakes in computer vision happen here, not in the models.

    You’ll be able to

    • Say what shape (480, 640, 3) means and index a pixel as img[y, x].
    • Crop a box (x1, y1, x2, y2) as img[y1:y2, x1:x2], and split colour channels.
    • Avoid swapped coordinates and uint8 overflow.

    Core ideas

    A colour image is a grid of pixels, stored as rows × columns × 3 channels (red, green, blue). Row 0 is the top. In these notebooks values run from 0 to 1; image files usually store whole numbers from 0 to 255, and adding to those wraps around past 255 instead of stopping. Convert to decimals before doing maths.

    The notebook opens with the whole game: about ten lines that find fish by how different they look from the background, draw boxes, and count. Then you change the water to “turbid harbour” and most of the fish disappear from the count.

    In the notebook

    The whole pipeline first, then arrays, channels and histograms, cropping and drawing, data types, grayscale and hue, and two checked exercises.

    An image has shape (480, 640, 3). How wide is it?

    640 pixels. Shape is height, width, channels.

    Why is red the darkest channel in an underwater frame?

    Water absorbs long red wavelengths fastest. Module 02 puts a number on it.

  2. 3 m
    02How water changes an imageoptional for your pathread the page›2 hThe physics behind the missing fish: attenuation, veiling light and detection range.

    Turbidity near an outfall changes what a camera can see, whatever is actually there. If you don’t model that, you will mistake the camera’s blindness for an ecological change.

    You’ll be able to

    • Write the image-formation model and explain each term.
    • Compute a detection range for a fish in a given water type.
    • Say what the project’s degrade() robustness utility assumes, and where it differs from the physics.
    • Explain why “restoring” a distant image mostly amplifies noise.

    Core ideas

    Light from a fish fades as it travels to the camera, separately for each colour, while particles in the water scatter ambient light into the lens:

    I = J · t + B · (1 − t),   t = e−βd for each channel

    J is the fish as it would look in air, B the water’s own colour, β how quickly that colour is lost per metre, and d the distance. As distance grows, t shrinks and every pixel drifts toward B: contrast vanishes no matter how bright the fish is. The explorer at the top of this page runs exactly this model.

    Inverting the model divides by t. When t is tiny, small errors such as sensor noise or 8-bit storage are multiplied enormously. Enhancement filters like CLAHE make frames easier to look at, but they can’t bring back detail that fell below the noise.

    From the project: a round-trip test of the image-formation model once failed at long range in turbid water because transmittance near 5 × 10−8 amplified rounding about 20 million times. The fix was a conditioning table, not a looser tolerance.

    In the notebook

    Transmittance curves for three water types, the same fish at 1 to 10 m, contrast and detection range, a best-fit comparison of degrade() with the physics, restoration error versus distance, enhancement and noise, low light, and an exercise on dark fish.

    A colleague says “we used CLAHE, so turbidity no longer affects our counts.” What do you ask?

    Whether they measured detection rate by turbidity after enhancement. Enhancement changes how a frame looks, not how much information it holds.

    Why does turbid coastal water often look green?

    Water absorbs red, and dissolved organic matter and particles absorb blue, so green survives best.

  3. 6 m
    03Classical image processingoptional for your path›2.5 hFilters, thresholds, shapes, and why touching fish break simple counts.

    These are the tools behind the aquaculture segmentation baseline and most fish counters. They’re transparent, fast and need no GPU, and their failures are easy to see.

    You’ll be able to

    • Write a filter from scratch and choose kernels for blur, sharpening and edges.
    • Pick a threshold automatically with Otsu’s method.
    • Clean a mask, split it into objects, and measure each object’s length with principal component analysis.

    Core ideas

    A filter slides a small grid of weights across the image and replaces each pixel with a weighted sum of its neighbours. Blurring averages, edge kernels subtract one side from the other. To find fish, measure how far each pixel’s colour is from the background colour, choose a threshold, remove specks with morphological opening, and group touching pixels into objects. The spread of each object’s pixels gives its orientation and length.

    Measured lengths come out a few percent short in the notebook, and more for small, distant fish, because edge pixels blend with the water. Touching fish merge into one object; a distance transform plus watershed can separate them, with care.

    In the notebook

    Convolution from scratch, Sobel edges, Otsu from scratch, morphology, connected components, PCA measurement against true lengths, watershed on touching fish, and a median background from video.

    Why blur before finding edges?

    Edge kernels amplify pixel noise. Blurring suppresses it first, at the cost of fine detail such as fins.

    Why is a per-pixel median better than a mean for a video background?

    Each fish covers a pixel only briefly, so the median ignores it; the mean gets pulled toward fish colours.

  4. 10 m
    04Learning to classify, without fooling yourselfoptional for your pathread the page›2.5 hFeatures, a classifier from scratch, honest splits, the right metrics, and saying “I don’t know”.

    The monitoring workstream’s first model sorts frames into fish, non-fish and empty. How you split and score it decides whether the reported number means anything.

    You’ll be able to

    • Train a softmax classifier on hand-made features.
    • Split video by clip, and show what goes wrong if you split by frame.
    • Read a confusion matrix; compute precision, recall and F1 per class.
    • Add an abstention rule and read the coverage trade-off.

    Core ideas

    Consecutive frames from one clip are near-duplicates. In the notebook, a nearest-neighbour classifier scores 0.90 when frames from the same clip land in both training and test sets, and 0.54 when whole clips are kept apart. The second number is the honest one. Split by clip, session, cage or site, never by frame.

    Precision asks: of the frames we called “fish”, how many were? Recall asks: of the real fish frames, how many did we find? On data that are mostly empty frames, a model that always answers “empty” can score 85% accuracy and find no fish at all.

    Below a confidence threshold the model answers unknown and sends the frame to a person. Fewer frames are decided, but the decided ones are more often right.

    Try it: the abstention trade-off

    In the notebook

    The leakage demonstration, features, softmax from scratch compared with scikit-learn, confusion matrix and per-class metrics, the imbalance trap, abstention, a reliability diagram, and an exercise on recall.

    A detector has precision 0.95 and recall 0.40. Describe it in plain words.

    When it says “fish” it’s usually right, but it misses most fish.

    Why standardise features using training statistics only?

    Using test statistics leaks information about the test set into training.

  5. 14 m
    05Robustness, shift and honest reportingoptional for your pathread the page›2 hOne number hides everything: report by condition and by site, and document limits.

    The project’s cameras won’t sit in the clear water a public dataset came from. A model’s behaviour in turbid, dark or unfamiliar water is the result that matters.

    You’ll be able to

    • Report accuracy for each condition and each habitat, not one overall figure.
    • Use augmentation and state what it did and didn’t fix.
    • Route unusable frames to human review with an image-quality gate.
    • Write a data card, a model card and a conservative event record.

    Core ideas

    In the notebook, the same model scores 0.93 on clean frames and 0.45 to 0.80 under turbidity, low light and blur, and 0.55 on fish that are physically farther away. Trained only on reef clips, it drops to 0.71 in a harbour. Training on degraded copies recovers much of the loss from degrade(), costs a little clean accuracy, and barely helps the farther fish: robustness to one degradation model doesn’t transfer to another.

    An image-quality gate scores brightness, texture and sharpness on the frame’s border, where there’s usually only background, so it judges the image, not the animal. Frames it flags become unknown and human_review_required, never a “safe” default, as in the Weeks 1–4 event schema.

    In the notebook

    Accuracy by condition, a habitat-shift table, augmentation, the quality gate, an error taxonomy, data and model cards, and event records. The exercise is the model card’s “must not be used for” field.

    Accuracy is 0.95 overall and 0.55 in turbid water. What’s the headline?

    Both, with the condition named. The overall figure alone misleads anyone deploying where turbidity is common.

    What should an event record say when the frame is degraded and confidence is 0.62?

    Event type unknown, action human_review_required.

  6. 18 m
    06Neural networks and CNNsoptional for your path›2.5 hA model that learns its own features, what it looks at, and a robustness test that can mislead.

    The monitoring workstream’s upgrade path is a convolutional network fine-tuned on real footage. You need to know what it adds, what it costs, and how to test it fairly.

    You’ll be able to

    • Train a linear model, a multilayer perceptron and a small CNN in PyTorch on CPU, and compare them by condition.
    • Inspect learned filters, feature maps and a saliency map.
    • Fine-tune a pretrained MobileNet on your own frames in Colab.

    Core ideas

    A CNN slides small learned filters across the image, so the same weights detect a fish anywhere in the frame. In the notebook it beats a multilayer perceptron with nearly 40 times more parameters. One preprocessing line, standardising each frame by its own mean and spread, lifts its clean accuracy from about 0.78 to 0.93.

    That line also lifts accuracy under degrade() turbidity and low light to around 0.93 to 0.99, because those distortions are nearly “multiply the frame by a number and add a constant”, which standardisation cancels. On fish that are physically farther away it stays near 0.7. A near-perfect score under one degradation model can hide a large gap under another. Test with more than one, and with real turbid footage.

    In the notebook

    PyTorch tensors, three models compared by condition, per-image normalisation, filters and feature maps, saliency, augmentation, size and latency, a tested transfer-learning function, and an exercise on layer shapes. About 2 minutes on a free CPU.

    A saliency map lights up the background, not the fish. What might be wrong?

    A shortcut: something in the background correlates with the label in your training data. Check the balance of classes across habitats and clips.

    Why freeze a pretrained network’s early layers at first?

    They hold general features such as edges and textures. Training only the new final layer first is faster and less likely to overfit a small dataset.

  7. 23 m
    07Detection, measurement and calibrationoptional for your path›2.5 hWhere is the fish, how long is it, how heavy is it, and how wrong could each answer be?

    Aquaculture growth and welfare evidence rests on length and mass estimated from images. Small measurement biases become large mass errors, and a badly built metric can look fine while carrying no information.

    You’ll be able to

    • Compute IoU, remove duplicate boxes, and score a detector with average precision.
    • Convert pixels to centimetres and explain why the scale depends on distance.
    • Quantify the size bias from a flat underwater camera port.
    • Propagate length error into mass error, and spot a circular welfare metric.

    Core ideas

    In the notebook the same detector scores an average precision of 0.99 in clear water, 0.71 in coastal water and 0.25 in a turbid harbour. In turbid water its recall never rises above about a quarter: no threshold finds fish that never produced a detection.

    Behind a flat port, light bends as it leaves the water, so fish look about 1.33 times larger than an in-air calibration expects, and more toward the edges of the frame. Mass is usually estimated as W = aLb with b near 3, so a 5% length error becomes roughly a 15% mass error.

    Try it: the flat-port bias

    A 30 cm fish 1 m away, thin port, camera square to the port. No ray beyond about 48.8° in water reaches the camera at all.

    For Capstone B: the aquaculture notebook estimates mass from length, then computes Fulton’s condition factor K = 100 W / L3 from that mass. With b = 3 this gives every fish K = 1.20, the exact lower edge of its placeholder band, so the welfare flag can’t respond to a thin or a fat fish. Module 07 shows why and what to do instead.

    In the notebook

    IoU, box formats, non-maximum suppression, precision–recall and average precision in three waters, COCO’s missing fish class, pixel scale and distance, flat-port refraction, length-to-mass error, the condition-factor problem with an image-based alternative, and a bootstrap biomass interval.

    Two equal boxes overlap by half their width. What’s their IoU?

    One third: the overlap is half a box, the union one and a half boxes.

    A camera calibrated in air is used behind a flat port. Are lengths too big or too small?

    Too big: by about a third at the centre, more toward the edges.

  8. 28 m
    08Tracking over timeoptional for your path›2.5 hFollowing individual fish across frames, and what the camera rig must guarantee.

    Counts, swimming behaviour and school cohesion all come from tracks. A tracker that splits one fish into five tracks inflates counts and corrupts every behaviour metric built on it.

    You’ll be able to

    • Implement a constant-velocity Kalman filter that coasts through missed detections.
    • Match detections to tracks with the Hungarian algorithm and a distance gate.
    • Report tracks per fish, ID switches and track purity.
    • Turn a tracking failure into a camera requirement.

    Core ideas

    The Kalman filter keeps a position and a velocity. Each frame it predicts where the fish should be, then blends that prediction with the new detection, trusting whichever is less uncertain. With no detection, it coasts on the prediction while its uncertainty grows.

    In the notebook’s simulation, 12 fish give 12 tracks when each moves less than a tenth of the distance to its nearest neighbour per frame, 32 tracks at about a quarter, and 60 at about half. That makes frame rate and field of view a rig requirement, not a tuning problem.

    From the project: a tracker once produced 205 tracks for 12 fish because simulated fish moved about 34 pixels per frame in a 37 cm field of view. It was documented as a rig requirement rather than tuned away.

    Try it: a Kalman filter through a turbid patch

    In the notebook

    A simulated school, a single-fish Kalman demo, a multi-fish tracker, tuning the gate and track lifetime, a camera-siting sweep, school polarisation from raw and Kalman velocities, line-crossing counts, and an exercise writing the polarisation metric.

    A tracker reports 40 tracks for 10 fish. Name two likely causes.

    Fish move too far between frames for the gate, or tracks are deleted too quickly during occlusions and turbid patches.

    Why can smoother velocities give a worse polarisation estimate?

    Smoothing lags behind turns, so lagged velocities point the wrong way when headings change fast. Noise and lag trade off; report which you saw.

  9. 34 m
    09From pixels to defensible claimsoptional for your pathread the page›2 hWhy fewer detections near an outfall doesn’t mean fewer fish, and how to write what the evidence supports.

    This is where camera results meet permits and policy. It’s the module every path ends with.

    You’ll be able to

    • Separate occupancy (is the animal there?) from detection probability (would the camera see it?) using repeat visits.
    • Explain false alarms and statistical power for a survey.
    • Write a conditional finding: what the evidence supports, under which assumptions, and what it doesn’t show.
    • Name the welfare, surveillance and licensing questions that come with the camera.

    Core ideas

    In the notebook’s simulated survey the outfall has no effect on fish at all, yet visits near it record a detection 41% less often, because the water is murkier. Visiting each site several times reveals how often a fish that is present gets missed, and an occupancy model uses that to estimate presence and detection separately.

    Repeated over 20 simulated surveys with no real impact, the naive comparison reported an impact 95% of the time and the occupancy model never did. When occupancy at the outfall really fell from 0.6 to 0.25, the model caught it in about a third of surveys: honest, and a reminder that “no detectable effect” means little without the survey’s power.

    The model only works because turbidity changes from visit to visit with weather and tide. If murkiness depended on distance alone, no analysis could separate it from a real effect. Survey design decides what can be known.

    Try it: seen versus there

    The real world is

    Long-run averages from the notebook’s model; any single survey adds noise. Units are illustrative.

    Responsibilities that come with the camera

    • Sentience. The UK’s Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022, an enacted law, recognises vertebrates (fish included), decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs as sentient for the purposes of that Act, following a 2021 evidence review led by Jonathan Birch at LSE. A model’s welfare flag is a reason for a person to look, never a diagnosis.
    • The rig can change the result. Lights, noise and equipment can change behaviour. Record the setup.
    • Surveillance. Tracking that counts fish can follow people on a deck or a shore. Ask who is recorded, whether they know, and how long footage is kept.
    • Licences. Record each dataset’s and each tool’s licence before use. Ultralytics YOLO is AGPL-3.0, which also covers models trained with it.
    • Policy accuracy. When findings are cited, keep enacted law, proposed bills and guidance clearly apart.

    In the notebook

    The no-impact world, the naive analysis, an occupancy model fitted by maximum likelihood, false alarms and power over 20 surveys, how earlier modules feed detection probability, a finding written automatically from the fitted numbers, and an exercise on survey effort.

    Detections fall by half near an outfall. Give two explanations other than fewer fish.

    Lower detection probability in murkier or darker water, fish avoiding the camera’s view but not the site, different survey effort, or a model that performs worse in those conditions.

    The interval for the outfall effect runs from −1.4 to +2.1. What can you say?

    The data are consistent with no effect, a negative one or a positive one. The survey can’t tell them apart; it needs more sites, more visits or better detection.

How the notebooks behave

Every notebook generates its own synthetic underwater data, so nobody needs a dataset or a GPU to start. All nine ran top to bottom without errors in testing: eight in under 10 seconds and Module 06 in under a minute on CPU. Real-data paths are present but switched off with RUN_REAL_DATA = False, as in the project notebooks.

Cells are marked ▶ run and read, ✏️ predict then change, and 🧩 write code. Each 🧩 exercise has a checker that prints feedback instead of crashing, followed by a collapsed solution.

Pacing

The full sequence is about 20 hours of student time. Two modules a week fits a five-week block, with capstones in the last week. Students on a single workstream path need about 12 to 15 hours.

What to look for in reports

  • Results by condition and by site, never one number.
  • Splits by clip or session, stated.
  • Robustness tested with more than one degradation model.
  • Placeholder values, such as species parameters and welfare bands, flagged as placeholders.
  • At least one conditional finding, with power or uncertainty stated.

Known simplifications

Scenes and surveys are synthetic. Attenuation coefficients and turbidity units are illustrative teaching values, not site measurements. Task numbers refer to the six-week versions of the project notebooks; tracking and occupancy-correction sections in later versions map to Modules 08 and 09.

Glossary

Abstention
Returning “unknown” below a confidence threshold, so a person reviews the frame.
Average precision (AP)
Area under a detector’s precision–recall curve. mAP averages it over classes or overlap thresholds.
Backscatter (veiling light)
Light scattered toward the camera by particles in the water, which washes out contrast.
Data leakage
Test data that shares information with training data, such as frames from the same clip, inflating scores.
Detection probability
The chance the camera detects an animal on a visit, given that the animal is there.
ID switch
A tracking error where one animal’s track jumps to another identity.
IoU
Intersection over union: overlap of two boxes divided by their combined area.
Kalman filter
A method that predicts an object’s next position and blends it with each new measurement by their uncertainties.
Occupancy
Whether a species uses a site, as distinct from whether it was detected there.
Power
The chance a survey detects an effect that really exists.
Precision and recall
Of the things called fish, the share that were; of the real fish, the share that were found.
Transmittance
The fraction of light of each colour that survives a given distance of water.
