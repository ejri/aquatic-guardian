A megawatt AI data centre cooled with seawater draws 0.84 cubic metres of water every second and sends it back °C warmer.

That is 19 million US gallons a day, 10 times the threshold at which US rules for cooling-water intakes at power plants and factories begin to apply. No data-centre reporting rule yet counts the fish, crabs and larvae drawn in with it.

AI's footprint reaches animals through water

Almost every watt a data centre draws leaves as heat. Where that heat is carried away by seawater, a river or a lake, or where the servers sit in the water itself, it reaches animals directly: fish and crabs pinned against intake screens, larvae drawn through heat exchangers, warm plumes that pull animals in and then chill them when a facility shuts down.

Governments are writing data-centre rules now, mostly about electricity prices, grids and communities. Aquatic Guardian gives them a ready-made module for the water and the animals: definitions, metrics, permit conditions and model clauses that fit the instruments already on the table.

Sentient Futures works on field building for the welfare of all sentient beings as AI reshapes the world. This project applies that mission to the physical side of AI. Compute governance usually means chips and training runs; here it means the heat and water of the buildings that run them, and the animals, many of them sentient, that live in that water. Aquatic Guardian grew out of the Sentient Futures fellowship, extending the Phase 1 report Governing the Ocean Server Farm into a framework, a toolkit and a public app.

The framework

Version 0.1, a public consultation draft. It adds to existing regulation rather than replacing it: every requirement names the instrument it attaches to.

  • Heat in water is pollution before it is a resourceUNCLOS counts the introduction of energy as marine pollution, and Canada's Fisheries Act already treats heated water as potentially deleterious.
  • Reuse only counts before dischargeHeat recovered from the cooling loop is reuse. Warm water released to a bay is discharge, whatever happens to it afterwards.
  • Critical conditions, not averagesMixing zones are judged at slack water and summer-maximum temperatures, as regulators already do for power plants.
  • Count individuals as well as populationsAnimals affected at intakes are reported by taxon, life stage and sentience evidence, never folded into a single score.
  • Honest uncertaintyScreening results are labelled as screening. Species values nobody has reviewed are marked as placeholders and cannot support a decision.
  • Indigenous rights firstSiting near Indigenous waters requires consultation to the applicable legal standard, including UNDRIP-aligned laws and treaty fishing rights.

  1. Disclosure

    Operators publish a machine-readable Aquatic Disclosure Record each period: heat to water, discharge temperature rise, intake flow and screen velocity, animals impinged and entrained by taxon and life stage, chemicals, noise, cables and freshwater use.

    Attaches to: DOE reporting in S.4214/H.R.9442, EU Delegated Regulation 2024/1364, state disclosure bills, BC Hydro applications.

  2. Assessment

    Before approval, a thermal study at critical design conditions with a recognised model, an intake assessment, a receptor assessment covering protected and sentient taxa, and cumulative climate effects.

    Attaches to: NPDES and §316(a) demonstrations, Fisheries Act authorisations, EU EIA and Habitats Directive assessments, CADA acceleration-zone designation.

  3. Permit conditions

    Closed-loop cooling by default, a numeric temperature increment at a defined mixing-zone edge, a 0.15 m/s screen velocity, cold-shock ramp-down, chemical limits and a reopener.

    Attaches to: NPDES permits, BC Environmental Management Act authorisations, member-state water permits.

  4. Monitoring

    Continuous temperature, summer oxygen, intake sampling by taxon and life stage, and occupancy surveys corrected for detection probability, published within 90 days.

    Attaches to: permit monitoring and reporting requirements.

  5. Adaptive management

    Exceedances, cold-shock events or detection-corrected declines trigger a reopener or load reduction. Data centres can shift compute to another site in minutes, which makes this far more practical than it ever was for power plants.

    Attaches to: permit reopeners, the EU 2028 rating review, BC's two-year allocation windows.

Screening tool

Estimate a facility's heat and water footprint, which rules it is likely to trigger, and what it would have to report. Everything runs in your browser; nothing you enter is sent anywhere.

How the numbers were checked

Every equation in the toolkit has a test that reproduces a published value or demonstrates the property on synthetic data with a known answer. The browser engine is tested against the Python reference on 200 random facilities.

Corrections adopted

  1. Per-port and per-length fluxes. Using total diffuser flow in a point-plume law understates dilution by N2/3, a factor of 4.6 for ten ports.
  2. Tidal excursion. Excursion is 2U/ω per half-cycle. The earlier 10.7 km implies a 0.75 m/s current, typical of straits rather than open coast.
  3. Numerical diffusion. First-order upwind at 25 m adds more mixing than the ocean does at any scale below about 3 km, so the old model under-predicted peak temperatures near the outfall.
  4. Critical conditions. Compliance is tested at slack water and summer maximum temperature, not at a mean current.
  5. Ammonia. Seawater speciation needs a salinity correction; US saltwater criteria date from 1989 and Canada has no marine guideline.
  6. Heat reuse. Reuse is practical only before discharge. Heat in open water is a regulated load, not a resource.

What still needs experts

Near-field dilution coefficients need a benchmark against Visual Plumes UM3 or NRFIELD. The seawater ammonia correction needs checking against the primary table. Only Washington and California carry a transcribed temperature rule; for British Columbia, the EU and Oregon the tool uses the increment you enter, which cannot show that a design complies. Species temperature thresholds and welfare parameters need species specialists and a fish-health veterinarian. Site currents must come from station data, and every tracker entry still needs a primary-source link.

The tool does not model stratified trapping, bottom attachment or far-field transport. Use it to scope a question, not to answer a permit.

Open source

The framework, model language, schemas, Python library, notebooks integration and this website live in one repository, so the policy text, the app and the research notebooks all use the same verified functions.

Code is Apache-2.0. Policy text and documentation are CC BY 4.0. The disclosure schema is CC0, so an agency can adopt it without conditions.
